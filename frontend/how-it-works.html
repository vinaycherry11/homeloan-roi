The question it answers

“Should I buy?” is really a comparison. The money you put into a home could have gone somewhere else. So the calculator asks: if you buy this home, will you end up richer than if you rented a similar one and invested the difference?

It answers by following both choices month by month, for as long as your loan runs, and comparing what each person owns at the end.

Two people, same money

Imagine two people with the same savings and the same monthly budget.

  • The buyer pays the down payment plus stamp duty and registration, takes the loan, and pays EMI and maintenance every month.
  • The renter puts that same upfront amount into an index fund (Nifty 50 by default, and you can pick others) and pays rent every month.

Both spend the same amount every month. Whoever's monthly cost is lower invests the gap in the fund. Usually that's the renter early on, because EMI plus maintenance is higher than rent. Later, as rent rises, it can flip to the buyer.

This is the fair way to compare. If the buyer's extra spending simply disappeared, buying would always look worse; if the renter spent the difference, renting would. Investing the gap keeps both people on the same budget.

What happens each month

1. EMI

The standard reducing-balance formula Indian banks use, with the annual rate split into twelve monthly ones:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)
P = loan amount, r = annual rate ÷ 12, n = months

Each month the interest is charged on the outstanding balance, and the rest of the EMI repays principal.

2. Maintenance

A percentage of the home's current value each year (0.5% by default), so it grows as the home appreciates. Taxes are not included.

3. Who invests the difference

gap = (EMI + maintenance) − rent
gap > 0 → the renter invests it
gap < 0 → the buyer invests it

4. Growth

Fund returns are quoted as yearly growth rates (CAGR), so the fund compounds at the equivalent monthly rate. Home price and rent each step up once a year by the rates you choose.

5. What each person owns

Buyer's wealth = home value − loan left + buyer's fund (if any)
Renter's wealth = renter's fund

Annual return and break-even

Annual return (IRR)

The single yearly rate that makes the buyer's cash flows add up to zero: the upfront payment going out, each month's (rent avoided − EMI − maintenance), and the home's value coming back at the end. If it's higher than the fund's rate, buying wins. That's exactly the same test as comparing the two people's final wealth.

Break-even price growth

The yearly home price growth at which both people end with exactly the same wealth. The calculator finds it by searching between −10% and +30%.

Break-even year

A simpler check that ignores what the money could have earned elsewhere: the first year when your equity plus the rent you didn't pay covers everything you've spent on the home.

What it doesn't model

  • Taxes of any kind: income-tax deductions on the loan, capital-gains tax on either side, or property tax.
  • Brokerage, selling costs, interiors and furnishing.
  • Changing interest rates, prepayments and loan transfers.
  • Vacant months, rent negotiations and moving costs.
  • Market ups and downs: both home prices and the fund grow at a steady rate.

Stamp duty and registration are capped at 7% of the price.

Data sources

Locality price growth

The city and locality presets use a blended 10-year trend (2014–2024) built from the RBI House Price Index, NHB Residex, Anarock Research, Knight Frank India and 99acres listings. No single official index covers every locality continuously, so the figures are estimates of the typical trend, not exact records. Individual buildings vary a lot.

Locality insights

Employers, schools, transit and amenities were compiled in June 2026 from 99acres, MagicBricks, Google Maps, JLL, Anarock and Knight Frank research.

Fund returns

The fund rates are long-run assumptions you can change, not forecasts: Nifty 50 12%, S&P 500 10.5%, Nifty Next 50 13.5%, Nifty Midcap 150 15%, large-cap mutual funds 13%, gold 9%, bank FD 7%, PPF 7.1%.
